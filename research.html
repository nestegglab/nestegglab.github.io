From fortunes to nest eggs

How families pass things on across generations, what has kept that inheritance in balance, and what the largest fortunes reveal about it.

How family fortunes last

Every family that lasts passes something on: land, knowledge, relationships, money.

For most of history that inheritance was held in check. It was shared out among heirs, spent down, taxed, or simply ended when a family line died out. Modern dynasty trusts and family offices are the first institutions designed to keep a fortune compounding indefinitely, by treating a family not as a household that divides its wealth every generation but as a lineage that never ends.

These structures also do real good: they keep family businesses whole, care for dependents and pass on habits of stewardship. The aim is to pump the brakes, not throw out the car, and to ask how the tools that hold the wealthiest families together could serve every family.

The full set of ideas behind this work is laid out in Guiding concepts.

Three moments, one questionInheritance from forager bands to family offices

A forager band shares out a hunt so no household gets far ahead. A medieval estate passes whole to the eldest son so the land is never divided. A modern family meets with its trustee to decide what the next generation will receive.

Each asks the same thing: what gets passed on, to whom, and what keeps it in balance? Families have also never lived alone. Bands traded, married and formed alliances with their neighbours, and wealthy families today do the same through marriage, shared investments, foundation boards and networks of family offices. Inheritance happens within families and between them.

What families pass onMoney is only part of it

Money

Material wealth passes most easily from one generation to the next, and it is what trusts are designed to hold.

Skills and stewardship

Knowledge, judgment and habits of care have to be rebuilt in every generation. Families that last tend to work at this deliberately.

Relationships and standing

Leadership of a family firm or foundation, board seats, a name, introductions. These are not divided among heirs and are not taxed, and may be the most durable inheritance of all.

A long view

US dynasty trusts and family offices are only three to five generations old. English records, which follow families for centuries, show status persisting far longer than any single parent-to-child link would suggest.

The brakesSix forces that have limited how long a fortune compounds
  • Division among heirsEach generation splits a fortune among more people. In many societies this has been the main brake on lasting wealth.
  • Lines that die outIn late medieval England about a quarter of noble families failed in the direct male line each generation, and their estates passed to daughters or cousins, often into other families. In the late Roman Republic only about a third of consuls had a son who also became consul.
  • Payouts to heirsIncome paid out and spent is no longer compounding.
  • Tax at each generationEstate and generation-skipping taxes apply when wealth passes down, unless it is sheltered.
  • Trusts that endTraditional trusts ended after a generation or two and divided their assets among heirs.
  • Costs along the wayIncome tax inside the trust, trustee fees and administration.
Getting around the brakesFrom entail to the dynasty trust

Every era has invented a way around division: primogeniture, entail, family foundations, and today the dynasty trust. A dynasty trust can be split into separate trusts for each branch of a family while the capital stays inside, sheltered from tax at each generation and still compounding.

Much of this is hard to see. Many modern trusts file little or nothing with tax authorities, and the US Treasury has said it has no data on the wealth held in domestic trusts. Measuring what official statistics miss is part of the work.

Nest eggs for every family

Whether the best of what wealthy families pass on (capital held for the long run, shared governance, teaching the next generation to steward it) can help ordinary families. Compounding itself is not the problem; concentration is. A few very large pools that never divide concentrate wealth, while many small ones spread it. This is an open question under test, not a finding.

How the work is doneModels, data and a public decision log

The research is meant to guide practical work with families, not to argue for particular policies. Models and data are built side by side: models show what a structure would do under stated assumptions, and data show what has actually happened. Each project records its predictions before results are run, reports its misses, and publishes its code, data sources and decision log.
