How family fortunes last

Our research studies how families pass things on across generations, what has kept that inheritance in balance, and what the largest fortunes reveal about it.

Our view

Every family that lasts passes something on: land, knowledge, relationships, money.

A forager band shares out a hunt so no household gets far ahead. A medieval estate passes whole to the eldest son so the land is never divided. A modern family meets with its trustee to decide what the next generation will receive. Three moments, one question: what gets passed on, to whom, and what keeps it in balance?

For most of history that inheritance was held in check. It was shared out among heirs, spent down, taxed, or simply ended when a family line died out. Modern dynasty trusts and family offices are the first institutions designed to keep a fortune compounding indefinitely. They do it by changing what a family is: not a household that divides its wealth every generation, but a lineage that never ends, with its capital held in one pool and its members as beneficiaries.

These structures also do real good. They keep family businesses whole, care for dependents, and pass on habits of stewardship along with money. We study how they work, what has kept inheritance in balance, and how the tools that hold the wealthiest families together could serve every family instead of a few.

What families pass on

Families have never lived alone. Bands traded, married and formed alliances with neighbouring bands, and much of what a family could pass on depended on those ties. Wealthy families today do the same through marriage, shared investments, foundation boards and networks of family offices. We look at inheritance within families and the dealings between them.

Money

Material wealth passes most easily from one generation to the next, and it is what trusts are designed to hold.

Skills and stewardship

Knowledge, judgment and habits of care have to be rebuilt in every generation. Families that last tend to work at this deliberately.

Relationships and standing

Leadership of a family firm or foundation, board seats, a name, introductions. These are not divided among heirs and are not taxed, and they may be the most durable inheritance of all.

A long view

US dynasty trusts and family offices are only three to five generations old. English records, which follow families for centuries, show status persisting far longer than any single parent-to-child link would suggest.

The brakes

Several forces have historically limited how long a fortune keeps compounding. We call them brakes. Our interest is in pumping the brakes, not throwing out the car: understanding which brakes still work, which have been removed, and what that means for families.

  • Division among heirsEach generation splits a fortune among more people. In many societies this has been the main brake on lasting wealth.
  • Lines that die outIn late medieval England about a quarter of noble families failed in the direct male line each generation, and their estates passed to daughters or cousins, often into other families. In the late Roman Republic only about a third of consuls had a son who also became consul.
  • Payouts to heirsIncome paid out and spent is no longer compounding.
  • Tax at each generationEstate and generation-skipping taxes apply when wealth passes down, unless it is sheltered.
  • Trusts that endTraditional trusts ended after a generation or two and divided their assets among heirs.
  • Costs along the wayIncome tax inside the trust, trustee fees and administration.

Every era has invented a way around division: primogeniture, entail, family foundations, and today the dynasty trust, which can be split into separate trusts for each branch of a family while the capital stays inside, sheltered and compounding. Many modern trusts also file little or nothing with tax authorities, and the US Treasury has said it has no data on the wealth held in domestic trusts. What we cannot see is part of what we study.

Research areas

Active

Trusts

What dynasty trusts do to wealth concentration, and what the historical record of American trusts shows: their size, payouts, costs and funding since the 1950s.

Hidden Dynasties (model, working paper). A companion study measuring the trust sector from tax and financial data is in progress.

Family offices

The organizations that manage large family fortunes: who runs them, what they cost, how they make decisions about trusts and payouts, and how visible they are in public data.

Ongoing

Inheritance across societies

How societies from forager bands to medieval nobility to modern America have passed things down, how families dealt with one another, which brakes held, and how families got around them.

In development

Nest eggs for every family

Whether the best of what wealthy families pass on (a pool of capital held for the long run, shared governance, teaching the next generation to steward it) can help ordinary families. Compounding itself is not the problem; concentration is. A few very large pools that never divide concentrate wealth, while many small ones spread it. This is a question we are testing, not a finding.

How we work

Our research is meant to guide practical work with families, not to argue for particular policies. We build models and assemble data side by side: models show what a structure would do under stated assumptions, and data show what has actually happened. Each project records its predictions before results are run, reports its misses, and publishes its code, data sources and decision log.
