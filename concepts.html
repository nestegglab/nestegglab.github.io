Guiding concepts

The ideas that shape the research: how inheritance works, the brakes that have kept it in balance, how families get around them, and what families pass on besides money.

Every family that lasts passes something on: land, knowledge, relationships, money.

Each concept below notes how strong the evidence is. Measured means from original data analysis. Documented means established in published research or official sources. Argued means a reasonable interpretation that is still debated. Open question means a working idea still being tested.

How inheritance works

Inheritance sets how much luck carries forwardThe engine behind long-run inequality
Documented

Each generation, luck opens new gaps between families. How closely children’s wealth follows their parents’ decides how much of that luck lasts. Long-run inequality settles where new luck balances the pull back toward the average.

Every brake weakens the link between generations. A trust that never ends, pays out little and escapes tax at each generation strengthens it.

Borgerhoff Mulder, Bowles and co-authors (2009); the model in Hidden Dynasties.

Three kinds of wealth pass on differentlyMaterial, embodied and relational
Documented

Material wealth (land, herds, money) passes most easily. Embodied wealth (skill, knowledge) and relational wealth (ties, standing) have to be rebuilt by each generation.

Trusts and family offices exist largely to make material inheritance independent of the heir’s own abilities.

Borgerhoff Mulder, Bowles and co-authors (2009); Gurven and co-authors (2010).

Innovation creates fortunes; rules decide whether they lastNew wealth and old wealth
Documented

New wealth mostly comes from building something: a firm, a technology, a market. Among the Forbes 400, the share who grew up wealthy fell from 60% in 1982 to 32% in 2011, and the share who started their own business rose from 40% to 69%. New fortunes competing with old ones is itself a brake.

Whether a fortune then lasts is set by law. Farming did not create lasting inequality by itself; defensible property and recognised inheritance rights did. Dynasty-trust laws change this second part, not the first.

Kaplan and Rauh (2013); Bowles and Choi (2019); Shennan (2011).

The brakes

Six brakes on dynastic wealthWhat has kept fortunes from compounding forever
Measured

Division among heirs, lines that die out, payouts to heirs, tax at each generation, trusts that end, and the taxes and costs along the way.

US trusts that file their own tax returns held a steady 3–4% of household wealth from 1954 to 2022. The brakes held for the part of the system that can be seen.

IRS Statistics of Income fiduciary returns, 1954–2022.

Division among heirs is the master brakeAnd how dynasty trusts divide only on paper
Documented in law; not yet measured

Any system of land inheritance needs a way to limit heirs, or the estate is quickly broken up. Equal division among sons left tiny, unworkable plots.

Dynasty trusts can divide without releasing anything. A trust can be split into separate trusts for each branch of a family, and each keeps its exemption from generation-skipping tax. In many states a trustee can also “decant” a trust into a new one with new terms. Division becomes a choice made by the trustee rather than a rule applied each generation. What is divided is the right to benefit, not the money.

Shennan (2011); 26 CFR 26.2642-6; Uniform Trust Decanting Act.

Lines die out, and fortunes move between familiesExtinction as a brake
Documented

In late medieval England about a quarter of noble families failed in the direct male line each generation. Their estates passed to daughters, cousins or their husbands, often into other families. In the last two centuries of the Roman Republic only about a third of consuls had a son who also became consul.

This brake often moved a fortune sideways into another family rather than ending it. A trust for “all descendants” removes it.

McFarlane (1973); Hopkins and Burton (1983).

Individual dynasties churn while the total holds steadyTurnover at the top
Documented

Famous family fortunes rise and fade, and wealth spreads across thousands of descendants, while the share of wealth held at the top can stay much the same. Turnover among families and a stable total fit together.

Korom and co-authors (2017).

Getting around the brakes

Every era invents a device to stop divisionFrom entail to the dynasty trust
Documented

Primogeniture, entail (from 1285), family-controlled churches and monasteries, the Fugger family settlements, American generation-skipping trusts, and today’s dynasty trusts. The modern trust is the latest in a long line, not a new invention.

Its relatives today are the other undivided pools: family partnerships and LLCs, family firms with controlling share classes, and private foundations. Each lets many relatives benefit while the capital stays in one piece.

Kades (2019), “Of Piketty and Perpetuities,” Boston College Law Review.

Great fortunes arrange not to be seenInvisibility, then and now
Documented

In 1516 Jakob Fugger agreed a fixed annual tax with the city of Augsburg so his family never had to disclose its wealth.

Today many grantor trusts file no separate tax return, and the US Treasury has said it has no data on the wealth held in domestic trusts.

Häberlein (2012); 26 CFR 1.671-4; US Treasury General Explanations, FY2025.

What families pass on besides money

Structure alone does not preserve a fortuneFamily practice matters
Argued

Some families built offices and trusts and stayed together; others built nothing and their fortunes dissipated. Legal structures seem to preserve wealth only when families also hold the meetings, keep the records and fill the roles.

Shiffer-Sebba (2025).

Heirs inherit stewardship, or don’tPreparing the children for the money
Documented

Wealthy families often describe inheritance as preparing the children for the money, and heirs tend to preserve rather than build. Whether stewardship can be taught deliberately is one of the central questions for ordinary families too.

Ethnographies of wealthy families, including Higgins (2022).

Relational wealth is the inheritance that doesn’t divideJobs, boards, names and standing
Open question

Much of what a family passes on is not shared among heirs: the top job in the family firm, the chair of the family foundation, board seats, a name, introductions. These go to one or a few heirs, are not taxed and do not dilute.

US dynasty trusts and family offices are only three to five generations old, so the long-run effects are still unfolding. England offers a longer view. Following rare surnames through probate records from 1858 to 2012, wealth persistence across whole families was far stronger than parent-to-child links alone suggest, and rich surnames of the 1850s still held 3.6 times average wealth around 2010.

Clark and Cummins (2015), Economic Journal.

Families interact the way bands didInheritance between families
Open question

Bands never lived alone. They traded, intermarried, formed alliances, split and merged, and a band’s standing depended on its ties to other bands. Wealthy families do the same through marriage, co-investment, shared foundation boards and networks of family offices.

Inheritance is not only what passes down inside a family but what moves between families, and the brakes may work through these ties.

A framing analogy guiding the work on inheritance across societies.
