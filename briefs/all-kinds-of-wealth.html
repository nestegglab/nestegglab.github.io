Counting every kind of family wealth

How business, national accounts and family-office practice record wealth that is not money, and a proposal for a family statement with three accounts: money, care and time, and what the family knows, holds together and stands for.

Richard Berk (ORCID 0009-0004-5344-4614) · Nest Egg Lab · November 2026 · Follows Brief 1 and the family financial statements concepts paper

Key pointsWhy money is not enoughHow others countLessonsThree accountsOpen questionsSources

Key points

  1. A family passes on more than money. The lab’s research distinguishes material, embodied and relational wealth. A statement of money alone shows the first and misses the other two.
  2. Others have tried to count more. Business has the triple bottom line and integrated reporting’s six capitals. National accounts have comprehensive wealth and a satellite account for unpaid household work. Family-office practice has “qualitative capital”.
  3. The common lesson is: separate accounts, not one number. The approaches that last keep each kind of capital in its own terms and avoid implying that a loss in one can be made up by a gain in another.
  4. Price only where a convention exists. Unpaid care can be given a replacement cost, as national statisticians do. Knowledge, relationships and purpose are better described than priced.
  5. Proposal: a core statement plus two satellite accounts. Money stays on the core statement under the lab’s draft rules. Care and time, and what the family knows, holds together and stands for, sit beside it in their own units.

1. Why money is not enough

The lab’s guiding concepts distinguish three kinds of inheritance. Material wealth, such as land and money, passes most easily. Embodied wealth (skill, knowledge, health) and relational wealth (ties, standing, a family’s name) have to be rebuilt in every generation. The concepts also note that family practice, not structure alone, seems to be what keeps a family’s wealth together.

The lab’s family financial statements are built for the first kind. They already record care, use of property and promises in a related-party schedule, but deliberately without prices. This brief asks how to give the other kinds of wealth a proper place on the statement without pretending they are money.

The triple bottom lineAnd its inventor’s recall

John Elkington coined the term in 1994. It asks that firms be judged on social, environmental and economic results, the “three Ps” of people, planet and profit. Elkington meant it to change how capitalism works. In 2018, near its 25th anniversary, he announced a “recall” of his own concept for rethinking, and later worked on how markets can address multiple capitals.

For families, the lesson is that a third and second bottom line are easy to name and hard to keep honest. Without rules for measuring them, they become slogans beside the one bottom line that has rules.

Documented
Integrated reporting’s six capitalsStocks of value an organisation uses and affects

The International Integrated Reporting Framework, first issued in 2013 and revised in 2021, is now maintained by the IFRS Foundation. It treats capitals as stocks of value that an organisation’s activities increase, decrease or transform, in six categories: financial, manufactured, intellectual, human, social and relationship, and natural. It is a set of principles rather than a measurement standard, and expects a mix of numbers and narrative.

Its most useful idea for families is the word stock. Each kind of capital is something that can grow or run down over time, not just a result for one year.

Documented
Comprehensive wealth of nationsThe World Bank and the problem of substitution

The World Bank’s Changing Wealth of Nations series measures a country’s wealth as produced, natural and human capital plus net foreign assets, as a check on whether growth can last. Human capital turned out to be the largest part of global wealth.

Its 2024 edition changed one assumption. Earlier editions implicitly treated all assets as substitutes, so a loss of forests could be made up by building roads. The new method allows for limited substitution, because some assets are becoming scarce. A family faces the same question: can money make up for care that is no longer given? The safe answer is not to assume it can.

Documented
Unpaid work in the national accountsA satellite account, kept beside GDP

GDP leaves out unpaid work at home. The US Bureau of Economic Analysis estimates it separately in a household production satellite account, using time-use surveys for hours and the wages of general-purpose domestic workers for value. Including it would have raised nominal GDP by 39% in 1965 and 26% in 2010.

Two ideas carry over directly. A satellite account adds information beside the core account without changing it. And there is a public, checkable convention for putting a price on hours of care when a family wants one.

Documented
Qualitative capital in family-office practiceWealth as well-being

James E. Hughes Jr., whose Family Wealth: Keeping It in the Family shaped modern family governance, argues that a family’s wealth is mainly its human and intellectual capital and only secondarily its money. His later work with Susan Massenzio and Keith Whitaker names five capitals: human, intellectual, social, legacy (or spiritual) and financial. Hughes has described a family balance sheet that lists these capitals as a humane tool for understanding a family.

This is the practice wealthy families already use. It has no common rules for evidence or measurement, which is the gap a public framework could fill.

Documented; practice, not research

3. Five lessons for a family statement

  • Separate accounts, no grand totalEach kind of wealth keeps its own unit. Nothing on the statement implies that money can make up for care or for relationships.
  • Stocks, and changes in themEach account shows where the family stands and what has grown or run down since the last statement.
  • Price only by a public conventionCare hours may carry a replacement cost by the national statisticians’ method, labelled as such. Nothing else is priced.
  • Evidence, even when it is not moneyHours from a diary, a skill someone has actually taught, a meeting that actually happened. The same rule as for money: say where each entry came from.
  • Satellites beside the coreThe money statement keeps its accounting rules. The other accounts sit beside it and never change its figures.

4. Proposal: one family statement, three accounts

AccountKind of wealthUnitWhat it recordsEvidence
Money (core)MaterialDollars, under ASC 274 and the lab’s draft rulesEach person’s statement, the related-party schedule, purpose-built accounts such as nest eggsStatements, deeds, forms
Care and time (satellite)Embodied, givenHours; optional replacement cost, labelledWho gives care to whom, how much, since when, what it replaces, any payment or promiseDiaries, calendars, the people involved
Knowledge, ties and purpose (satellite)Embodied and relationalDescriptions and yes/no indicatorsWho knows how to do what; who holds which roles; what the family does together; what it says it stands forRecords, meeting notes, the family’s own words

The third account can be kept short and checkable with a few plain questions, each answered yes, no or not yet, with the evidence noted:

  • Does more than one person know where everything is? (Form 1 exists and is shared.)
  • Has the family met about money or care in the last year? (A meeting record exists.)
  • Has each adult been taught to read their own statement?
  • Does each nest egg have someone, other than its controller, who understands it?
  • Has the family written down what it wants its money to be for?

The questions measure practice, not virtue. They can be answered from records, they change over time, and they point to what to do next. That fits the lab’s nest-egg question: whether the practice of wealthy families, not only their capital, can help ordinary ones.

Open question

5. Open questions

  • Should care ever carry a price?A price makes care comparable to money and may help fairness between siblings. It may also turn care into a bargaining chip. The proposal makes it optional and labelled.
  • Who answers the third account?Each person, the family together, or both? Different answers from different members may be the most useful information.
  • Can it be tested?The lab’s method calls for predictions recorded before results. A test would ask whether families keeping all three accounts make decisions the decision owner recognises as theirs, more often than families keeping money alone.
  • What stays private?The same rule as money: each entry is shared only if the people in it agree.
