What wealth asks of its owners

For most of history, wealth came with demands: work, obligations to other people, and ties to a place. Modern instruments are designed to ask almost nothing. This brief asks what that changes, whether the old demands acted as a brake on dynastic wealth, and how light obligations might be put back by choice.

Key points

  1. Wealth used to make demands. Among foragers, having more meant being asked to share. A landed estate needed running, tenants, marriages and an heir who lived there. Owning tied a person to work, to other people and to a place.
  2. Modern wealth asks almost nothing of its owner. A diversified portfolio is managed by professionals, passes by a beneficiary form, and belongs to no place. The work, the ties and the place have been designed out.
  3. The demands may have been a brake. Sharing, supporting dependants and running an estate wore fortunes down and tied them to people. The lab proposes this as a seventh brake on dynastic wealth, to be tested.
  4. The old demands were not all good. Many were imposed by elders, husbands or lords. The question is which obligations are worth keeping and who chooses them, not a return to the past.
  5. Light obligations can be chosen. The lab’s open tools already ask a little: say what money is for, record what passes between family members, review it once a year. Where a trust is used, model terms can write such practices down, with a set end date as a deliberate brake.

1. Three moments

The lab’s research follows inheritance through three moments: forager bands, landed estates, and the family offices and trusts of today. Asking what wealth required of its owner in each gives a different view of the same history.

Forager bandsLanded estatesModern instruments
What wealth wasFood, tools, skills and, above all, ties to other peopleLand, a house, a name, dependantsDiversified portfolios, trusts, retirement accounts
Work it askedHunting, gathering and sharing outRunning the estate; managing tenantsAlmost none from the owner; professionals do it
Ties it askedOthers could demand a share, and refusal had a costHeirs, marriages, retainers, duties to tenants and the parishA beneficiary form, a trustee, an adviser
Place it askedThe band’s rangeThe estate itselfNone
How it wore downShared away on demandDivided among heirs, spent on obligations, or lost when a line died outPayouts and taxes, where they apply

Bands. Anthropologists once described forager sharing as generosity. Peterson showed that much of it is demand sharing: people ask, and those who have are expected to give. Demand sharing is a way of testing and confirming social ties in egalitarian societies. Having more created claims on you.

People as wealth. In much of African history, wealth was counted in followers, dependants and kin, a pattern historians call wealth in people. Guyer and Belinga refined this: people were valued as holders of particular knowledge as well as numbers of dependants. Wealth of this kind cannot be held without the relationships it consists of.

Estates. The lab’s guiding concepts describe the devices that kept estates whole, from primogeniture and entail to family settlements. Those devices also bound the heir: to the land, to the marriages that protected it, and to the dependants who lived on it.

Polanyi described the modern shift in general terms: land, labour and money, once bound up with social relations and community rules, came to be treated as commodities. Wealth held as financial assets is the furthest point of that movement.

Documented for bands and people as wealth; argued as a pattern

2. Wealth that asks nothing

Three examples, at three scales:

  • A family office with diversified investmentsThe work of owning has moved to professionals. Harrington’s study of wealth managers, based on 65 interviews in 18 countries, shows a profession that uses trusts, offshore structures and shell companies to shield fortunes from tax and from other legal obligations. The owner’s remaining task is to supervise the people who do the work.
  • A dynasty trust with one beneficiaryThe beneficiary need do nothing to receive. The trust deed decides, the trustee administers, and the family’s ties to each other are optional. Marcus’s anthropological study of American dynastic families, Lives in Trust, examines families whose lives are organised around such arrangements.
  • A retired couple’s IRA, far from familyOrdinary wealth has followed the same path. A beneficiary form decides who inherits, overriding the will. The money and the family no longer need to be in the same place, and care and money often travel separately.

In each case something is gained. Owners are freed from work they may not want or be good at, and independence from family obligation is, for many people, the point. The question is what is lost alongside it.

Documented for the examples; argued as a pattern

3. A seventh brake?

The lab’s research identifies six brakes that historically kept inherited wealth in check: payouts to heirs, tax at each generation, trusts that end, taxes and costs, division among heirs, and lines dying out. The demands of wealth may be a seventh, and the oldest: sharing, supporting dependants and running an estate all wore fortunes down, and all tied them to people.

If that is right, modern instruments remove two things at once. They remove a brake on accumulation, and they separate money from the relationships and practices the lab’s concepts suggest hold families together.

Hypotheses, to be tested:

  1. Wealth that asks less of its owner lasts longer as money.
  2. Wealth that asks less of its owner does less to hold a family together.
  3. Families that keep chosen practices around their money (meeting, reviewing, teaching) keep more relational wealth, whatever the size of the money.
Hypothesis

4. Cautions

  • Obligations were often imposedThe old demands came with control. Primogeniture favoured one son; estates bound daughters through marriage; historical household accounts were sometimes used to restrain women’s spending. The lab’s rule against romanticising foragers applies to estates too.
  • Distance can be freedomA retired couple living far from their children may be choosing independence, not suffering a loss. The framework should record what a family chooses, not judge it.
  • Conditions written into trusts have a poor recordTrusts that reward or punish behaviour, often called incentive trusts, are criticised as inflexible and as control by the dead: their authors cannot foresee every circumstance, and the terms can become a burden. Any obligation the lab proposes should be light, chosen and revisable.

5. Putting light obligations back

6. Model terms for a nest-egg trust

Where a donor or a family uses a trust, its terms are the place to write such obligations down. Dynasty trusts use that tool to remove brakes. A nest-egg trust could use it to put light ones back. The terms below are a starting point for lawyers to adapt, not a legal document, and they are deliberately independent of any service provider.

TermModel approachWhy
BeneficiaryOne person per trust, or one separate share per person under a common agreementKeeps each nest egg distinct; separate single-beneficiary shares can also matter for generation-skipping tax when beneficiaries are much younger than the donor
How gifts enterFor adults, a short, notified right to withdraw each gift; for minors, a trust that the child can take at 21, continuing if they leave it inThe established routes for gifts in trust to count toward the annual gift tax exclusion
PurposeStated purposes, such as education, a first home, starting a business, retirement, or care for familyWrites the Purpose tag into the deed
InvestmentLow-cost, broadly diversified index fundsMirrors the rule Congress wrote for Trump Accounts; keeps the trustee’s task simple
End dateThe trust ends and pays out by a set age, such as 30 or 35A deliberate brake: a nest egg, not a dynasty
DistributionsFor the stated purposes, at the trustee’s discretion; no conditions on marriage, religion, weight or similarAvoids the worst of incentive trusts
PracticesAn annual statement to the beneficiary in the lab’s Form 2 format, and an annual conversation with the trustee; encouraged, not enforcedLight obligations, chosen rather than imposed
TrusteeIndependent of the donor’s and any provider’s commercial interestsNo side, as in the lab’s proposed requirements
ExitThe withdrawal window stays real; a beneficiary who takes the money out is not penalisedRespects the owner; how many stay in is itself worth studying

Trusts for many small gifts carry real costs: a trustee, annual notices and tax filings for each beneficiary. A common agreement with pooled administration may be the only way such trusts make sense at nest-egg scale. Gift, generation-skipping and income tax treatment all need a lawyer’s review for any real use. None of this is legal or tax advice.

Open question

7. How to test it

As in the lab’s other work, predictions are recorded before results are run, and misses are reported.

  • Across historyCompare how long fortunes lasted, and how often families stayed connected, where wealth carried heavy obligations and where it carried few. The lab’s anthropology line and trust-sector data are the starting points.
  • Among families todayCompare families who take on the light practices above with families who do not: whether they meet, whether care and money are discussed together, whether the decision owner recognises decisions as theirs.
  • Among nest eggsWhere gifts are made in trust with a withdrawal window, record how many recipients leave the money in, and whether the practices are kept.
